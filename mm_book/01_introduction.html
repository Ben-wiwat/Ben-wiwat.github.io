Chapter 1 Introduction to Mathematical Modelling

Mathematical modelling is the process of representing real-world systems using mathematical language. The objective is to develop mathematical equations that capture the essential behaviour of physical, biological, engineering or social systems so that they can be analysed, understood and used to make predictions.

A mathematical model provides a simplified description of reality. Rather than attempting to represent every detail of a physical system, a model retains only those features that are relevant to the problem under investigation.

Typical applications include

  • population growth,
  • heat transfer,
  • traffic flow,
  • fluid dynamics,
  • disease transmission,
  • water filtration,
  • structural mechanics.

The development of a mathematical model usually involves making assumptions, identifying governing physical principles, deriving mathematical equations, solving the equations, and interpreting the results in the context of the original problem.

1.1 What is Mathematical Modelling?

A mathematical model is a mathematical representation of a real-world system.

The objective of modelling is not to reproduce reality exactly, but to capture the essential features required to answer a particular scientific or engineering question.

1.2 Variables, Parameters and State Variables

A mathematical model typically contains:

  • Independent variables: quantities that describe the domain of the problem (e.g. time, space).
  • Dependent variables: quantities whose values depend on the independent variables.
  • Parameters: fixed quantities that characterise the system.
  • State variables: variables describing the current state of the system.
TipExample: Newton’s Cooling Law

\[ \frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T-T_a), \] where

  • \(t\) is time (independent variable),
  • \(T(t)\) is the temperature of the object at time \(t\) (dependent/state variable),
  • \(T_a\) is the ambient temperature (parameter),
  • \(k\) is a positive constant that depends on the properties of the object and the surrounding environment (parameter).

1.3 Why Build Mathematical Models?

Mathematical models are used to

  • Understand physical mechanisms.
  • Predict future behaviour.
  • Test scientific hypotheses.
  • Design engineering systems.
  • Optimise performance.
  • Reduce experimental costs.

Examples include

  • Predicting temperature distributions in a reactor.
  • Estimating contaminant transport in groundwater.
  • Forecasting traffic congestion.
  • Designing aircraft wings.

1.4 The Mathematical Modelling Process

Mathematical modelling is an iterative process rather than a single calculation. The modelling cycle consists of several stages.

The modelling cycle may require several iterations before the mathematical model adequately represents the real system.

TipExample: Cooling Coffee

Problem: Determine how the temperature of a cup of coffee changes over time.

Assumptions:

  • Coffee is perfectly mixed.
  • Ambient temperature remains constant.
  • Heat loss is proportional to the temperature difference.

Model Formulation: \[ \frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T-T_a), \] where

  • \(T(t)\) is the coffee temperature,
  • \(T_a\) is the ambient temperature,
  • \(k>0\) is a cooling constant.

Solution:

\[ T(t) = T_a+(T_0-T_a)e^{-kt}. \]

1.5 Physical Systems

A physical system is the portion of the universe selected for investigation.

Examples include

  • a moving vehicle,
  • a heat exchanger,
  • a chemical reactor,
  • a river,
  • a traffic network,
  • a biological cell.

Everything outside the system is called the surroundings or environment.

The interaction between the system and its surroundings occurs through its boundary.

1.5.1 System Boundary

The boundary separates the system from its surroundings.

Boundaries may be

  • fixed,
  • moving,
  • real,
  • imaginary.

Across the boundary there may be transfer of

  • mass,
  • momentum,
  • energy.

Choosing an appropriate system boundary is one of the most important steps in constructing a mathematical model.

1.5.2 Control Volumes

Many engineering systems involve the flow of fluids through pipes, channels, heat exchangers and reactors.

Instead of following individual particles, we consider a fixed region in space, called a control volume.

The boundary enclosing the control volume is called the control surface.

Typical examples include

  • a water tank,
  • a pipe section,
  • a pump,
  • a combustion chamber.

The control-volume approach forms the basis of fluid mechanics, heat transfer, chemical engineering and environmental modelling.

1.6 Assumptions and Idealisations

Every mathematical model involves simplifications.

George Box famously remarked: “All models are wrong, but some are useful.”

Common assumptions include:

  • Neglect air resistance.
  • Constant density.
  • Uniform temperature.
  • One-dimensional geometry.
  • Steady-state conditions.

1.7 Dimensional Analysis

TipExample:

  • Velocity \[ v=\frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}, \quad \text{so}\quad [v]=LT^{-1}.\]

  • Acceleration \[ a=\frac{\text{velocity}}{\text{time}}, \quad \text{so}\quad [a]=LT^{-2}.\]

1.7.1 Dimensional Consistency

Every term in a physical equation must possess the same dimensions.

TipExample: Dimensional consistency of the equation of motion

\[ s=ut+\frac12 at^2. \]

Dimensions:

\[ [L] = [LT^{-1}][T] + [LT^{-2}][T^2]. \]

Hence,

\[ [L]=[L]+[L], \]

which confirms dimensional consistency.

1.8 Scaling and Characteristic Quantities

Complex systems often involve multiple length and time scales.

TipExample: Characteristic timescale of a vehicle

If a vehicle travels a distance \(L\) with characteristic speed \(U\), then the characteristic timescale is

\[ T_c=\frac{L}{U}. \]

For \(L=1000\ \mathrm{m},\;U=25\ \mathrm{m/s},\) we obtain

\[ T_c=40\ \mathrm{s}. \]

Scaling arguments allow engineers and scientists to identify dominant physical processes and simplify mathematical models.
