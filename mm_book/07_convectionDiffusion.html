Chapter 7 Convection-Diffusion Process and Applications

This section develops the scalar transport equation for a generic conserved quantity \(c(x,t)\) (e.g. concentration of a solute, temperature, dye intensity), clarifies the underlying assumptions, and presents canonical examples with closed-form solutions.

7.1 Governing equations for convection-diffusion

Diffusion transport of mass

Diffusion is a process in which mass is transported from regions of higher concentration to regions of lower concentration. Typical examples include the spread of pollution in the atmosphere, changes in salinity in water, or heat transfer by conduction.

Let \(C\) be the concentration (volume density) of a solute. If the distribution is not uniform, mass flows down the concentration gradient. states that the diffusive mass flux is proportional to the gradient of concentration: \[\begin{equation} \mathbf q_d \;=\; -\,D\,\nabla C, \tag{7.1} \end{equation}\] where \(D>0\) is the diffusivity.

Convection (advection) transport of mass

Transport of mass via bulk motion of the fluid is called convection (or advection). If \(\mathbf v\) is the fluid velocity, the convective mass flux is \[\begin{equation} \mathbf q_c \;=\; C\,\mathbf v. \tag{7.2} \end{equation}\]

Remark. The equations governing diffusive/convective flux are analogous to those for heat flux by conduction/convection.

Balance, fluxes, and the PDE

Consider a control volume \(V\subset\Omega\) with outward unit normal \(\mathbf n\) and boundary \(\partial V\). Conservation of \(c\) reads \[ \frac{d}{dt}\int_V C\,dV = -\int_{\partial V} \underbrace{\mathbf j\cdot\mathbf n}_{\text{net outflux}}\,d\Gamma + \int_V Q\,dV, \] where \(Q\) is a volumetric source (production \(>0\); consumption \(<0\)) and the total flux \(\mathbf j\) is the sum of diffusive and convective parts: \[ \mathbf j = \mathbf q_d + \mathbf q_c = \underbrace{-\,\mathbf D\,\nabla C}_{\text{diffusion (Fick/Fourier)}}\ + \underbrace{C\,\mathbf v}_{\text{convection/advection}} . \] Here \(\mathbf D(x)\) is a (possibly anisotropic) diffusivity tensor; \(\mathbf v(x,t)\) is the carrier velocity (for heat, \(\mathbf v\) is a fluid velocity in forced convection; in pure conduction \(\mathbf v\equiv\mathbf 0\)).

Applying the divergence theorem and localizing gives the convection–diffusion equation \[\begin{equation} \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \nabla\!\cdot(C\,\mathbf v) = \nabla\!\cdot(\mathbf D\,\nabla C) + Q \quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,t_f]. \tag{7.3} \end{equation}\] For incompressible flow (\(\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf v=0\)) this reduces to

\[\displaystyle C_t + \mathbf v\!\cdot\nabla C = \nabla\!\cdot(\mathbf D\,\nabla C)+Q.\] Typical boundary conditions on \(\Gamma=\partial\Omega\) are \[ \text{Dirichlet: } C=C_D,\qquad \text{Neumann: } -\mathbf n\,\,\!\cdot\!\,\,(\mathbf D\nabla C) = \bar q,\qquad \text{Robin: } -\mathbf n\,\,\!\cdot\!\,\,(\mathbf D\nabla C)=k\,(C-C_\infty). \]

Analogies. For heat transport,

  • \(C\mapsto T\) (temperature),

  • \(\mathbf D\mapsto \alpha=\dfrac{k}{\rho c_p}\) (thermal diffusivity),

  • \(Q\mapsto \dfrac{q''' }{\rho c_p}\),

  • Fourier’s law: \(\mathbf q_c=-k\nabla T\) replaces \(-\,\mathbf D\nabla C\).

For solute transport in liquids or gases,

  • \(\mathbf D\) is the molecular (or turbulent) diffusivity,
  • and \(Q\) accounts for reactions or sources.

Initial and boundary conditions.

To close a PDE model we supply an initial condition

  • For unsteady problems, the initial concentration distribution is specified by \[ C(\mathbf{x},0)=C_0, \quad \mathbf{x} \in \Omega \] and appropriate boundary conditions (Dirichlet/Neumann/Robin types) on \(\Gamma=\partial\Omega\).

  • For steady problems, boundary conditions alone suffice.

7.2 Non-dimensionalization and key parameters

Let \(C^\ast, L, U\) be characteristic scales for \(C\), length and speed, and \(t^\ast=L/U\). Introduce \[ \hat C=\frac{C}{C^\ast},\quad \hat{\mathbf x}=\frac{\mathbf x}{L},\quad \hat t=\frac{t}{t^\ast},\quad \hat{\mathbf v}=\frac{\mathbf v}{U},\quad \hat{\mathbf D}=\frac{\mathbf D}{D_0}. \] Then (7.3) becomes (dropping hats) \[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \nabla\!\cdot(C\,\mathbf v) = \frac{1}{\mathrm{Pe}}\;\nabla\!\cdot(\hat{\mathbf D}\,\nabla C) + \tilde Q, \] where \(\displaystyle\mathrm{Pe}=\frac{UL}{D_0} \quad\text{(Péclet number)}.\)

For a 1-D problem with constant \(v\) and \(D\), the dimensionless equation is \[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \mathrm{Pe}\,\left(v\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2} + \tilde Q. \]

For a 2-D problem with constant \(v\) and \(D\), the dimensionless equation is \[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \mathrm{Pe}\,\left(v_x\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial x} + v_y\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial y}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial y^2} + \tilde Q. \]

For a 3-D problem with constant \(v\) and \(D\), the dimensionless equation is \[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \mathrm{Pe}\,\left(v_x\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial x} + v_y\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial y} + v_z\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}\right) \] \[ = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial z^2} + \tilde Q. \]

7.3 Applications

Physical examples of diffusion and convection-diffusion processes include:

  • Molecular solute in water at room T: \(D\sim 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\).
  • Gas-phase diffusion (air): \(D\sim 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\).
  • Thermal diffusivity of liquids/solids: \(\alpha\sim 10^{-7}\)–\(10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\).
  • Microchannel mixing (\(U\sim 10^{-3}\)–\(10^{-1}\) m/s, \(L\sim 10^{-4}\)–\(10^{-3}\) m): \(\mathrm{Pe}\) often \(10^2\)–\(10^5\) \(\Rightarrow\) advection-dominated.
  • Groundwater plumes (\(U\sim 10^{-6}\)–\(10^{-4}\) m/s, \(L\sim 10\)–\(10^3\) m): dispersion (effective \(D\)) \(10^{-6}\)–\(10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\).

7.3.1 Analytical solutions in 1-D

Let \(x\in(0,L)\), \(D>0\), \(v\) constant, \(Q=0\) unless stated.

Example - Pure diffusion, semi-infinite domain (step input).

For \(x>0\), \(t>0\), \[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D\,\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2} \] subject to \[ C(x,0)=0,\quad C(0,t)=C_0,\quad C(\infty,t)=0. \] Solution (error-function profile): \[ \boxed{\ C(x,t)=c_0\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{D t}}\right). \ } \] The penetration depth grows like \(\delta\sim\sqrt{Dt}\) and the total mass in the domain grows like \(M(t)\sim\sqrt{Dt}\).

The error-function solution is a similarity solution with variable \(\eta=x/(2\sqrt{Dt})\), where \[ \mathrm{erfc}(\eta)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_\eta^\infty e^{-s^2}\,ds. \]

The complementary error function \(\mathrm{erfc}(\eta)\), together with the complementary error function \(\mathrm{erf}(\eta)=1-\mathrm{erfc}(\eta)\), is a standard special function in diffusion problems.

Calculate the total mass in the domain:

\[ M(t)=\int_0^\infty C(x,t)\,dx = C_0 \int_0^\infty \mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{D t}}\right)\,dx \] \[ = 2 C_0 \sqrt{D t} \int_0^\infty \mathrm{erfc}(u)\,du = C_0 \sqrt{\pi D t}. \]

Example - Advection–diffusion, semi-infinite (moving front).

\[ C_t + v\,C_x = D\,C_{xx},\qquad C(x,0)=0,\qquad C(0,t)=C_0,\qquad C(\infty,t)=0. \] Galilean shift \(\,\xi=x-vt\,\) yields \[ \boxed{\ C(x,t)=C_0\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x-vt}{2\sqrt{D t}}\right). \ } \] A diffusive front convects with speed \(v\) and spreads with \(\sqrt{Dt}\).

Example - Advection–diffusion with impulse (Green’s function).

With initial pulse \(C(x,0)=M\,\delta(x-x_0)\) and zero boundary flux, \[ \boxed{\ C(x,t)=\frac{M}{\sqrt{4\pi D t}}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-x_0-vt)^2}{4Dt}\right). \ } \] A Gaussian packet convects and diffuses; mass \(M\) is conserved.

Example - Steady 1-D advection–diffusion between two boundaries.

\[ D\,C'' - v\,C' = 0,\qquad C(0)=C_0,\quad C(L)=C_L. \] Let \(\mathrm{Pe}=vL/D\). The solution is \[ \boxed{\ C(x)=C_0 + (C_L-C_0)\, \frac{e^{\mathrm{Pe}\,x/L}-1}{e^{\mathrm{Pe}}-1}. \ } \] As \(\mathrm{Pe}\!\to\!0\), \(C(x)\) tends to the linear profile \(C_0+(C_L-C_0)\,x/L\); for large \(\mathrm{Pe}\!>\!0\) a thin layer forms near \(x=L\) (outflow boundary).

Wall mass transfer (Robin/film model).

  • At \(x=0\), \(\quad-D\,C_x = k_m\,(C-C_\infty)\);

  • At \(x=L\), \(\quad C(L)=C_L\).

For \(Q=0\) and constant \(v,D,k_m\), the steady solution is \[ C(x)=C_L + \frac{(C_0-C_\infty)-\beta(C_L-C_\infty)}{1-\beta} \left(1-\frac{e^{\mathrm{Pe}\,x/L}-1}{e^{\mathrm{Pe}}-1}\right) \] with \(\displaystyle\beta=\frac{k_m L}{D}\,\frac{1}{\mathrm{Pe}},\) which reduces to a linear wall law as \(v\to0\).

Example - Cooling slab, pure conduction

A semi-infinite solid \(x\in(0,\infty)\), initially at uniform temperature \(T_i\), is exposed at \(x=0\) to a fluid at temperature \(T_\infty\). Heat is extracted at the surface by convection with heat–transfer coefficient \(h\ge 0\). The solid has constant conductivity \(k\), density \(\rho\), heat capacity \(c_p\), and thermal diffusivity \(\displaystyle \alpha=\frac{k}{\rho c_p}\).

Find \(T(x,t)\) such that \[\begin{align} \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} &= \alpha\,\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}, && x>0,\ t>0, \\ -k\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}(0,t) &= h\big(T(0,t)-T_\infty\big), && t>0, \\ \lim_{x\to\infty} T(x,t) &= T_\infty, && t>0, \tag{7.3}\\ T(x,0) &= T_i, && x>0. \end{align}\]

Let \(\theta(x,t)=T(x,t)-T_\infty,\quad \theta_i=T_i-T_\infty,\)

For a cooling solid occupying \(x>0\), the physically consistent convection condition is

\[ k\theta_x(0,t)=h\theta(0,t) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \theta_x(0,t)=\beta\theta(0,t), \] where \(\displaystyle\beta=\frac{h}{k}.\)

Thus, we have the Robin boundary condition at \(x=0\) and the Dirichlet condition at \(x\to\infty\).

We solve

\[\begin{align} \theta_t &=\alpha\theta_{xx},\qquad x>0,\ t>0,\\ \theta_x(0,t)&=\beta\theta(0,t),\tag{7.4}\\ \theta(\infty,t)&=0,\\ \theta(x,0)&=\theta_i. \end{align}\]

The solution is derived entirely by separation of variables + generalized Fourier expansion. By assumption, the solution is of the form

\[ \theta(x,t)=X(x)G(t). \]

Substitution gives

\[ XG'=\alpha X''G, \]

so

\[ \frac{G'}{\alpha G} = \frac{X''}{X} =-\lambda. \]

Thus

\[ G'+\alpha\lambda G=0 \]

and

\[ X''+\lambda X=0. \]

For the continuous spectrum, put

\[ \lambda=\mu^2,\qquad \mu>0. \]

Then

\[ G(t)=e^{-\alpha\mu^2t} \]

and

\[ X''+\mu^2X=0. \]

The general spatial solution is

\[ X(x)=A\cos\mu x+B\sin\mu x. \]

Its derivative is

\[ X'(x)=-A\mu\sin\mu x+B\mu\cos\mu x. \]

At (x=0),

\[ X(0)=A,\qquad X'(0)=B\mu. \]

The Robin condition requires

\[ X'(0)=\beta X(0), \]

hence

\[ B\mu=\beta A, \]

or

\[ B=\frac{\beta}{\mu}A. \]

We may therefore choose the eigenfunction

\[ \boxed{ X_\mu(x)= \cos\mu x+\frac{\beta}{\mu}\sin\mu x }. \]

Hence an individual separated solution is

\[ \theta_\mu(x,t) = \left( \cos\mu x+\frac{\beta}{\mu}\sin\mu x \right)e^{-\alpha\mu^2t}. \]

Because \(0<x<\infty\), \(\mu\) is not discrete. Therefore we superpose all values of \(\mu\).

2. Fourier integral solution

Write

\[ \theta(x,t) = \int_0^\infty A(\mu) \left( \cos\mu x+\frac{\beta}{\mu}\sin\mu x \right) e^{-\alpha\mu^2t}\,d\mu . \]

At \(t=0\),

\[ \theta_i = \int_0^\infty A(\mu) \left( \cos\mu x+\frac{\beta}{\mu}\sin\mu x \right)d\mu. \]

This is the generalized Fourier expansion associated with the Robin boundary condition.

The eigenfunctions satisfy the generalized orthogonality relation

\[ \int_0^\infty X_\mu(x)X_\nu(x)\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2} \left(1+\frac{\beta^2}{\mu^2}\right) \delta(\mu-\nu). \]

Consequently, for a function \(f(x)\),

\[ A(\mu) = \frac{2}{\pi} \frac{1}{1+\beta^2/\mu^2} \int_0^\infty f(x) \left( \cos\mu x+\frac{\beta}{\mu}\sin\mu x \right)dx. \]

Here

\[ f(x)=\theta_i. \]

Thus

\[ A(\mu) = \frac{2\theta_i}{\pi} \frac{\mu^2}{\mu^2+\beta^2} \left[ \int_0^\infty\cos\mu x\,dx + \frac{\beta}{\mu} \int_0^\infty\sin\mu x\,dx \right]. \]

For \(\mu>0\), in the generalized Fourier sense,

\[ \int_0^\infty\cos\mu x\,dx=0, \]

and

\[ \int_0^\infty\sin\mu x\,dx=\frac1\mu. \]

Therefore

\[ A(\mu) = \frac{2\theta_i}{\pi} \frac{\mu^2}{\mu^2+\beta^2} \frac{\beta}{\mu^2}. \]

Hence

\[ A(\mu) = \frac{2\theta_i\beta} {\pi(\mu^2+\beta^2)} \]

3. Fourier-integral representation

Substituting \(A(\mu)\),

\[ \theta(x,t) = \frac{2\theta_i\beta}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{ \displaystyle \cos\mu x+\frac{\beta}{\mu}\sin\mu x } {\mu^2+\beta^2} e^{-\alpha\mu^2t}\,d\mu. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \frac{\theta(x,t)}{\theta_i} = \frac{2\beta}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \frac{ \mu\cos(\mu x)+\beta\sin(\mu x) } {\mu(\mu^2+\beta^2)} e^{-\alpha\mu^2t}\,d\mu. \]

This is the answer obtained directly by separation of variables and Fourier expansion.

Because the domain is semi-infinite, this is technically a Fourier integral rather than a Fourier series.

4. Evaluation of the Fourier integral

The integral can be evaluated in terms of the error functions.

Introduce

\[ \eta=\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}, \qquad \gamma=\beta\sqrt{\alpha t} = \frac{h\sqrt{\alpha t}}{k}. \]

The Fourier integral evaluates to

\[ \frac{\theta(x,t)}{\theta_i} = \operatorname{erf}(\eta) + e^{\,2\eta\gamma+\gamma^2} \operatorname{erfc}(\eta+\gamma). \]

Since

\[ 2\eta\gamma = 2\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right) \left(\frac{h\sqrt{\alpha t}}{k}\right) = \frac{hx}{k}, \]

and

\[ \gamma^2 = \frac{h^2\alpha t}{k^2}, \]

we obtain

\[\begin{align} \frac{\theta(x,t)}{\theta_i} =&\,\, \operatorname{erf}\left( \frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}} \right)\\ &+ \exp\left( \frac{hx}{k} + \frac{h^2\alpha t}{k^2} \right) \operatorname{erfc}\left( \frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}} + \frac{h\sqrt{\alpha t}}{k} \right). \end{align}\]

Finally,

\[ \theta=T-T_\infty, \qquad \theta_i=T_i-T_\infty, \]

so

\[\begin{align} T(x,t)=T_\infty&+ (T_i-T_\infty) \left[ \operatorname{erf}\left( \frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}} \right)\right.\\ &+ \left. \exp\left( \frac{hx}{k} +\frac{h^2\alpha t}{k^2} \right) \operatorname{erfc}\left( \frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}} +\frac{h\sqrt{\alpha t}}{k} \right) \right]. \end{align}\]

5. Verification of the initial condition

At \(t\to0^+\), for a fixed \(x>0\),

\[ \frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\to\infty. \]

Therefore

\[ \operatorname{erf} \left( \frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}} \right)\to1, \]

while the second term tends to zero. Hence

\[ \frac{\theta(x,0)}{\theta_i}=1, \]

so

\[ T(x,0)=T_i. \]

At \(x\to\infty\) and any \(t>0\),

\[ \theta(x,t)\to0, \]

and therefore

\[ T(x,t)\to T_\infty. \]

Note. About the minus sign in the original question

Your problem statement writes

\[ -k\theta_x(0,t)=h\theta(0,t). \]

For \(x>0\), this sign corresponds mathematically to

\[ X'(0)=-\beta X(0), \]

and the spatial eigenfunctions become

\[ X_\mu(x) = \cos\mu x-\frac{\beta}{\mu}\sin\mu x. \]

More importantly, an additional eigenfunction appears:

\[ X_b(x)=e^{-\beta x}, \qquad \lambda=-\beta^2, \]

whose time dependence is \(\displaystyle e^{+\alpha\beta^2t}\).

Thus the solution contains an exponentially growing mode, which is inconsistent with the stated physical situation of cooling. This is why the usual convection-cooling boundary condition for a half-space \(x>0\) is

\[ kT_x(0,t)=h[T(0,t)-T_\infty]. \]

So the Fourier-integral solution above is the standard separation-of-variables solution for the intended cooling semi-infinite slab.

Example - 1D Steady River Pollutant Transport

Let \(C(x)\) be the cross-sectionally averaged concentration along a long straight reach (\(x>0\)). Assume constant mean velocity \(v\) (taken positive downstream) and constant axial diffusivity \(D\). Neglect sources/sinks and time dependence. A common 1-D steady advection–diffusion model for the excess \(C-C_\infty\) is \[\begin{equation} D\,C''(x)+v\,C'(x)=0,\qquad x>0, \tag{7.5} \end{equation}\] together with the boundary conditions \[\begin{equation} C(0)=C_0,\qquad \lim_{x\to\infty}C(x)=C_\infty . \tag{7.6} \end{equation}\]

Sign convention:

  • The conservative steady balance can be written as \[ \dfrac{d}{dx}\!\big(vC-D C'\big)=0, \] which, after expanding, yields \[ vC'-DC''=0. \]
  • Reversing the \(x\)-axis (or equivalently letting \(v\mapsto -v\) produces (7.5).
  • The exponential downstream of an inlet condition occurs with (7.5).

Derivation of the exponential profile

Let \(y(x):=C(x)-C_\infty\) so that \(y(\infty)=0\). Then (7.5) becomes \[\begin{equation} D\,y''+v\,y'=0. \tag{7.7} \end{equation}\] Set \(p=y'\). Equation (7.7) gives \(D\,p'+v\,p=0\), hence \[ \frac{p'}{p}=-\frac{v}{D}\quad\Rightarrow\quad p(x)=K\,e^{-(v/D)x}. \]

Integrating once, \[ y(x)=\int p(x)\,dx = -\,\frac{KD}{v}\,e^{-(v/D)x}+A. \] The far-field condition \(y(\infty)=0\) forces \(A=0\). The inlet condition \(y(0)=C_0-C_\infty\) determines \(-KD/v=C_0-C_\infty\). Therefore \[ C(x)=C_\infty+(C_0-C_\infty)\,e^{-(v/D)\,x}. \]

Characteristic equation (alternative)

Seeking \(y\sim e^{rx}\) in (7.7) yields \[ D r^2 + v r=0\Rightarrow r\in\{0,-v/D\}$. \]

Hence, \[ y(x)=A + B e^{-(v/D)x}. \] Boundedness at \(+\infty\) gives

\[ A=0; \quad B=C_0-C_\infty. \]

Checks and interpretation

With \(C(x)\) above, \[ C' = -\tfrac{v}{D}(C_0-C_\infty)e^{-(v/D)x} \] and \[ C'' = \tfrac{v^2}{D^2}(C_0-C_\infty)e^{-(v/D)x}, \] so \[ D C''+v C' = (v^2/D)-(v^2/D)=0. \]

E-folding length. The solution decays exponentially with characteristic length \[ \ell= \frac{D}{v}. \]

After a distance \(\ell\) the inlet excess has decayed to \(1/e\): \[ \big(C(\ell)-C_\infty\big)/(C_0-C_\infty)=e^{-1}. \]

Dimensionless form and Péclet number.

Let \(\xi=x/\ell=x\,v/D\). Then \[ \frac{C(x)-C_\infty}{C_0-C_\infty}=e^{-\xi}. \] On a finite reach of length \(L\), the Péclet number is \(\mathrm{Pe}=vL/D\), and \[ \big(C(L)-C_\infty\big)/(C_0-C_\infty)=e^{-\mathrm{Pe}}. \]

Flux viewpoint.

The total axial flux \(j=vC-DC'\) is generally not constant in \(x\) under (7.5). In fact, for the decaying solution, \[ j'=vC'-DC''=2vC'\neq 0. \] The conservative steady form \[ \frac{d}{dx}(vC-DC')=0 \] corresponds to the opposite sign in the differential equation and, with \[ C(\infty)=C_\infty \] on a semi-infinite domain, yields the trivial constant solution unless additional physics (reaction, lateral exchange) is included.

Common extensions include lateral exchange with a reservoir, first-order decay, and time-dependent inlet conditions. These lead to more complex models that can still be analyzed using similar techniques.

(i) First-order decay (reaction) with rate \(\kappa>0\).

Model \(-\kappa\,(C-C_\infty)\) as a volumetric sink: \[ D\,y''+v\,y'-\kappa\,y=0,\qquad y=C-C_\infty. \] Characteristic roots \(r=\dfrac{-v\pm\sqrt{v^2+4\kappa D}}{2D}\) give the bounded solution \[ C(x)=C_\infty+(C_0-C_\infty)\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{x}{\ell_\kappa}\right), \] where the e-folding length is \[ \ell_\kappa=\frac{2D}{\,v+\sqrt{v^2+4\kappa D}\,}\;<\;\frac{D}{v}. \]

(ii) Finite interval \(x\in[0,L]\) with Dirichlet ends.

Solve \(D C''+v C'=0\) with \(C(0)=C_0\), \(C(L)=C_L\): \[ C(x)=C_0+(C_L-C_0)\, \frac{e^{-(v/D)\,x}-1}{e^{-(v/D)\,L}-1}. \]

(iii) Robin inlet with semi-infinite domain (compatibility)

Take \[ -D C'(0)=k_m\,[C(0)-C_\infty]\quad\text{for}\quad C(\infty)=C_\infty, \] we have \[ C(x)=C_\infty+B e^{-(v/D)x}, \] the inlet condition becomes \[ v\,B=k_m\,B, \] so a nontrivial exponential (\(B\neq 0\)) requires \(k_m=v\) [m/s]. Otherwise the only bounded solution is \(C\equiv C_\infty\). Nontrivial Robin problems on a semi-infinite domain usually arise when additional sinks (e.g. reactions or lateral exchange) are present.

Bottom line Under (7.5)–(7.6), the solution is an exponential decay of the inlet excess concentration with characteristic length \(D/v\): \[ C(x)=C_\infty+(C_0-C_\infty)\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{v}{D}\,x\right), \qquad \ell=\frac{D}{v}. \] A conservative tracer is injected at \(x=0\); far downstream \(C\to C_\infty\). Take \(v>0\), \(D\) constant. The steady ODE \[ D C'' - v C' = 0, \] with \(C(0)=C_0\) and \(C(\infty)=C_\infty\), gives \[ C(x)=C_\infty + (C_0-C_\infty)\,\exp\left(-\frac{v}{D}\,x\right). \]

The e-folding length \(D/v=L/\mathrm{Pe}\) characterizes downstream dilution.

Extensions to include reactions, finite domains, and Robin boundary conditions can be handled similarly.

7.3.2 Analytical solutions in 2-D

For anisotropic media (e.g. crystals, layered composites), tensor diffusion is described by a symmetric positive-definite matrix \(\mathbf D\): \[ \mathbf D=\text{R}(\theta) \begin{bmatrix}D_1&0\\0&D_2\end{bmatrix} \text{R}(\theta)^\top \] with rotation \(\text{R}(\theta)\).

Example - 2-D diffusion in a rectangle

Consider a rectangular domain \(0<x<L\), \(0<y<H\) with constant isotropic diffusivity \(D>0\). The governing equation is \[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D\,\left(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial y^2}\right),\quad t>0, \] with initial condition \(C(x,y,0)=f(x,y)\) and boundary conditions such as \[ C(0,y,t)=f_1(y,t),\quad C(L,y,t)=f_2(y,t), \] \[ C(x,0,t)=f_3(x,t),\quad C(x,H,t)=f_4(x,t). \]

Solution methods include separation of variables, Fourier series, and Green’s functions, depending on the boundary conditions.

For the rectangular diffusion problem

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D\left( \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial y^2} \right), \qquad 0<x<L,\quad 0<y<H, \]

the cleanest separation-of-variables solution is obtained first for homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. General nonzero boundary data can then be handled by subtracting a function that satisfies the prescribed boundary values.

1. Homogeneous boundary conditions

Consider first

\[ C(0,y,t)=C(L,y,t)=0, \]

\[ C(x,0,t)=C(x,H,t)=0, \]

with

\[ C(x,y,0)=f(x,y). \]

Assume a separated solution

\[ C(x,y,t)=X(x)Y(y)T(t). \]

Substituting into the PDE,

\[ XYT' = D\left(X''YT+XY''T\right). \]

Divide by (DXYT):

\[ \frac{1}{D}\frac{T'}{T} = \frac{X''}{X} +\frac{Y''}{Y}. \]

Since the left-hand side depends only on \(t\), while the right-hand side depends on \(x,y\), each part must be constant. Let

\[ \frac{X''}{X}=-\lambda, \qquad \frac{Y''}{Y}=-\mu. \]

Then

\[ \frac{T'}{DT}=-(\lambda+\mu). \]

Thus the separated ODEs are

\[ X''+\lambda X=0, \]

\[ Y''+\mu Y=0, \]

\[ T'+D(\lambda+\mu)T=0. \]

2. \(x\)-direction eigenvalue problem

The boundary conditions are

\[ X(0)=0,\qquad X(L)=0. \]

For nontrivial solutions,

\[ \lambda_n=\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots \]

and

\[ \boxed{ X_n(x)=\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) }. \]

3. \(y\)-direction eigenvalue problem

Similarly,

\[ Y(0)=0,\qquad Y(H)=0 \]

gives

\[ \mu_m=\left(\frac{m\pi}{H}\right)^2, \qquad m=1,2,3,\ldots \]

and

\[ \boxed{ Y_m(y)=\sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) }. \]

4. \(t\)-dependent part

The time equation becomes

\[ T' + D \left[ \left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{m\pi}{H}\right)^2 \right]T =0. \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{ T_{nm}(t) = \exp\left\{ -D\left[ \left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{m\pi}{H}\right)^2 \right]t \right\}. } \]

Therefore one separated solution is

\[ C_{nm} = \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) e^{-D[(n\pi/L)^2+(m\pi/H)^2]t}. \]

By superposition, the general solution is the double Fourier sine series

\[ \boxed{ C(x,y,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} A_{nm} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) e^{-D[(n\pi/L)^2+(m\pi/H)^2]t}. } \]

5. Determine the Fourier coefficients

At \(t=0\),

\[ f(x,y) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} A_{nm} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right). \]

Using orthogonality,

\[ \int_0^L \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{p\pi x}{L}\right)dx = \begin{cases} 0,&n\ne p,\\[2mm] L/2,&n=p, \end{cases} \]

and similarly,

\[ \int_0^H \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \sin\left(\frac{q\pi y}{H}\right)dy = \begin{cases} 0,&m\ne q,\\[2mm] H/2,&m=q. \end{cases} \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{ A_{nm} = \frac{4}{LH} \int_0^L\int_0^H f(x,y) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \,dy\,dx. } \]

Therefore, for zero boundary values,

\[ \begin{aligned} C(x,y,t) &= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \left[ \frac{4}{LH} \int_0^L\int_0^H f(\xi,\eta) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi \xi}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi \eta}{H}\right) \,d\eta\,d\xi \right]\\ &\quad\times \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \exp\left[ -D\pi^2 \left( \frac{n^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2}{H^2} \right)t \right]. \end{aligned} \]

This is the standard 2-D Fourier-series solution.

6. Nonhomogeneous boundary conditions

Now suppose

\[ C(0,y,t)=f_1(y,t),\qquad C(L,y,t)=f_2(y,t), \]

\[ C(x,0,t)=f_3(x,t),\qquad C(x,H,t)=f_4(x,t). \]

Introduce

\[ \boxed{ C(x,y,t)=u(x,y,t)+v(x,y,t), } \]

where \(v\) is chosen to satisfy the nonhomogeneous boundary conditions. Then \(u\) satisfies homogeneous boundary conditions.

For example, a convenient boundary-lifting function can be constructed in the form

\[ v(x,y,t) = \left(1-\frac{x}{L}\right)f_1(y,t) + \frac{x}{L}f_2(y,t) + \left(1-\frac{y}{H}\right)g_3(x,t) + \frac{y}{H}g_4(x,t), \]

where \(g_3\) and \(g_4\) are adjusted to avoid double-counting the corner values.

Then

\[ u=C-v. \]

Substituting into the diffusion equation gives

\[ u_t = D(u_{xx}+u_{yy}) + F(x,y,t), \]

where

\[ \boxed{ F(x,y,t) = D(v_{xx}+v_{yy})-v_t. } \]

The boundary conditions for (u) are homogeneous:

\[ u(0,y,t)=u(L,y,t)=0, \]

\[ u(x,0,t)=u(x,H,t)=0. \]

Therefore expand \(u\) as

\[ u(x,y,t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{m=1}^\infty a_{nm}(t) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right). \]

Similarly, expand the forcing term:

\[ F(x,y,t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{m=1}^\infty F_{nm}(t) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right), \]

where

\[ F_{nm}(t) = \frac{4}{LH} \int_0^L\int_0^H F(x,y,t) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \,dy\,dx. \]

Substitution gives

\[ \frac{da_{nm}}{dt} + D\pi^2 \left( \frac{n^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2}{H^2} \right) a_{nm} = F_{nm}(t). \]

Let

\[ \lambda_{nm} = D\pi^2 \left( \frac{n^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2}{H^2} \right). \]

Then

\[ a_{nm}'+\lambda_{nm}a_{nm}=F_{nm}(t), \]

whose solution is

\[ a_{nm}(t) = a_{nm}(0)e^{-\lambda_{nm}t} + \int_0^t e^{-\lambda_{nm}(t-\tau)} F_{nm}(\tau)\,d\tau. \]

The initial coefficients follow from

\[ u(x,y,0)=f(x,y)-v(x,y,0), \]

so

\[ a_{nm}(0) = \frac{4}{LH} \int_0^L\int_0^H [f(x,y)-v(x,y,0)] \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \,dy\,dx. \]

Finally,

\[ C(x,y,t) = v(x,y,t) + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} a_{nm}(t) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right). \]

For the especially common case of zero concentration on all four boundaries, \(v=0\), and the solution reduces directly to the double Fourier sine series derived above.

Example - 2-D diffusion in a rectangle with mixed boundary conditions

Consider diffusion in the rectangular domain

\[ 0<x<L,\qquad 0<y<H, \]

with constant diffusivity \(D>0\). The concentration \(C(x,y,t)\) satisfies

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D\left( \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial y^2} \right), \qquad t>0. \]

As a representative example of mixed boundary conditions, suppose the concentration is prescribed to be zero on the vertical boundaries, while the horizontal boundaries are impermeable:

\[ C(0,y,t)=0,\qquad C(L,y,t)=0, \]

and

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial y}(x,0,t)=0, \qquad \frac{\partial C}{\partial y}(x,H,t)=0. \]

The initial concentration is prescribed as

\[ C(x,y,0)=f(x,y). \]

Thus, the complete problem is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} C_t &=D(C_{xx}+C_{yy}), &&0<x<L,\quad 0<y<H,\quad t>0,\\ C(0,y,t)&=0, &&0<y<H,\\ C(L,y,t)&=0, &&0<y<H,\\ C_y(x,0,t)&=0, &&0<x<L,\\ C_y(x,H,t)&=0, &&0<x<L,\\ C(x,y,0)&=f(x,y). \end{aligned} } \]

The conditions at \(x=0\) and \(x=L\) are Dirichlet conditions, while those at \(y=0\) and \(y=H\) are Neumann conditions. Therefore, this is a mixed-boundary-value diffusion problem.

1. Separation of variables

Assume a separated solution of the form

\[ C(x,y,t)=X(x)Y(y)T(t). \]

Substitution into the diffusion equation gives

\[ X(x)Y(y)T'(t) = D \left[ X''(x)Y(y)T(t) + X(x)Y''(y)T(t) \right]. \]

Dividing by \(DXYT\) gives

\[ \frac{1}{D}\frac{T'}{T} = \frac{X''}{X} + \frac{Y''}{Y}. \]

The left-hand side depends only on \(t\), while the right-hand side depends only on \(x\) and \(y\). Consequently, both sides must be constant.

Introduce two separation constants \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) such that

\[ \frac{X''}{X}=-\lambda, \]

and

\[ \frac{Y''}{Y}=-\mu. \]

Then

\[ \frac{1}{D}\frac{T'}{T} = -(\lambda+\mu). \]

The original PDE therefore reduces to the three ordinary differential equations

\[ X''+\lambda X=0, \]

\[ Y''+\mu Y=0, \]

and

\[ T'+D(\lambda+\mu)T=0. \]

We now solve each eigenvalue problem separately.

2. Solution in the \(x\)-direction

The \(x\)-dependent equation is

\[ X''+\lambda X=0, \]

with boundary conditions

\[ X(0)=0, \qquad X(L)=0. \]

Consider \(\lambda>0\) and write

\[ \lambda=p^2. \]

Then

\[ X''+p^2X=0, \]

whose general solution is

\[ X(x)=A\cos(px)+B\sin(px). \]

Using the boundary condition at \(x=0\),

\[ X(0)=A=0. \]

Therefore,

\[ X(x)=B\sin(px). \]

The second boundary condition gives

\[ X(L)=B\sin(pL)=0. \]

For a nontrivial solution \(B\ne0\), we require

\[ \sin(pL)=0. \]

Thus,

\[ pL=n\pi, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots \]

and therefore

\[ p_n=\frac{n\pi}{L}. \]

Hence the eigenvalues are

\[ \boxed{ \lambda_n= \left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots } \]

and the corresponding eigenfunctions are

\[ \boxed{ X_n(x)= \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right). } \]

Notice that the Dirichlet boundary conditions naturally produce a Fourier sine series in the \(x\)-direction.

3. Solution in the \(y\)-direction

The \(y\)-dependent equation is

\[ Y''+\mu Y=0, \]

with Neumann boundary conditions

\[ Y'(0)=0, \qquad Y'(H)=0. \]

For \(\mu>0\), write

\[ \mu=q^2. \]

Then

\[ Y''+q^2Y=0, \]

and the general solution is

\[ Y(y)=A\cos(qy)+B\sin(qy). \]

Differentiating,

\[ Y'(y) = -Aq\sin(qy) + Bq\cos(qy). \]

At \(y=0\),

\[ Y'(0)=Bq=0. \]

For \(q\ne0\),

\[ B=0. \]

Thus

\[ Y(y)=A\cos(qy). \]

Applying the condition at \(y=H\),

\[ Y'(H) = -Aq\sin(qH)=0. \]

For a nontrivial solution,

\[ \sin(qH)=0. \]

Hence

\[ qH=m\pi, \]

so that

\[ q_m=\frac{m\pi}{H}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \mu_m= \left(\frac{m\pi}{H}\right)^2. } \]

There is an important difference from the Dirichlet problem: the Neumann problem permits

\[ m=0. \]

When \(m=0\),

\[ \mu_0=0, \]

and the corresponding differential equation is

\[ Y''=0. \]

Its general solution is

\[ Y=A+By. \]

The Neumann conditions require \(B=0\), giving the constant eigenfunction

\[ Y_0=1. \]

Thus the complete set of \(y\)-direction eigenfunctions is

\[ \boxed{ Y_m(y) = \cos\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right), \qquad m=0,1,2,\ldots } \]

with eigenvalues

\[ \boxed{ \mu_m= \left(\frac{m\pi}{H}\right)^2, \qquad m=0,1,2,\ldots } \]

The Neumann boundary conditions therefore give a Fourier cosine series in the \(y\)-direction.

4. Solution of the time equation

For the modes \(n\) and \(m\), the time equation becomes

\[ T_{nm}' + D(\lambda_n+\mu_m)T_{nm}=0. \]

Substituting the eigenvalues,

\[ T_{nm}' + D \left[ \left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{m\pi}{H}\right)^2 \right] T_{nm} =0. \]

Separating variables,

\[ \frac{dT_{nm}}{T_{nm}} = -D \left[ \left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{m\pi}{H}\right)^2 \right]dt. \]

Integrating,

\[ \ln T_{nm} = -D \left[ \left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{m\pi}{H}\right)^2 \right]t + \text{constant}. \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ T_{nm}(t) = \exp \left[ -D \left( \frac{n^2\pi^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2\pi^2}{H^2} \right)t \right]. } \]

5. Individual separated solutions

Combining \(X_n\), \(Y_m\), and \(T_{nm}\) gives

\[ C_{nm}(x,y,t) = A_{nm} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \exp \left[ -D\pi^2 \left( \frac{n^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2}{H^2} \right)t \right]. \]

Because the diffusion equation is linear, an arbitrary linear combination of these modes is also a solution.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ C(x,y,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} A_{nm} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \exp \left[ -D\pi^2 \left( \frac{n^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2}{H^2} \right)t \right]. } \]

The remaining task is to determine \(A_{nm}\) from the initial condition.

6. Apply the initial condition

At \(t=0\),

\[ C(x,y,0)=f(x,y). \]

Therefore,

\[ f(x,y) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} A_{nm} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right). \]

This is a mixed two-dimensional Fourier expansion: a sine series in \(x\) and a cosine series in \(y\).

To determine the coefficients, we use orthogonality.

7. Orthogonality in the \(x\)-direction

The sine functions satisfy

\[ \int_0^L \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{r\pi x}{L}\right) dx = \begin{cases} 0, & n\ne r,\\[6pt] \dfrac{L}{2}, & n=r. \end{cases} \]

Thus,

\[ \int_0^L X_n(x)X_r(x)\,dx = \frac{L}{2}\delta_{nr}. \]

8. Orthogonality in the \(y\)-direction

For \(m,q\geq1\),

\[ \int_0^H \cos\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \cos\left(\frac{q\pi y}{H}\right) dy = \begin{cases} 0, &m\ne q,\\[6pt] \dfrac{H}{2}, &m=q. \end{cases} \]

However, the zero mode \(m=0\) must be treated separately because

\[ Y_0(y)=1. \]

Therefore,

\[ \int_0^H Y_0^2\,dy=H, \]

whereas for \(m\geq1\),

\[ \int_0^H Y_m^2\,dy=\frac{H}{2}. \]

Consequently, the coefficients for \(m=0\) and \(m\geq1\) have slightly different normalization factors.

9. Fourier coefficients for \(m=0\)

For the zero \(y\)-mode, multiply the initial expansion by

\[ \sin\left(\frac{r\pi x}{L}\right) \]

and integrate over the rectangle.

The result is

\[ \boxed{ A_{n0} = \frac{2}{LH} \int_0^L \int_0^H f(x,y) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \,dy\,dx. } \]

The \(m=0\) contribution is therefore

\[ C_0(x,y,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} A_{n0} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \exp\left( -\frac{Dn^2\pi^2}{L^2}t \right). \]

This part of the solution is independent of \(y\).

10. Fourier coefficients for \(m\geq1\)

For \(m\geq1\), orthogonality gives

\[ \boxed{ A_{nm} = \frac{4}{LH} \int_0^L \int_0^H f(x,y) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \,dy\,dx, \qquad m\geq1. } \]

Therefore, the complete solution may be written as

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} C(x,y,t) ={}& \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} A_{n0} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) e^{-Dn^2\pi^2t/L^2} \\ &+ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} A_{nm} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \\ &\qquad\times \exp \left[ -D\pi^2 \left( \frac{n^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2}{H^2} \right)t \right]. \end{aligned} } \]

where

\[ \boxed{ A_{n0} = \frac{2}{LH} \int_0^L \int_0^H f(\xi,\eta) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi \xi}{L}\right) \,d\eta\,d\xi, } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ A_{nm} = \frac{4}{LH} \int_0^L \int_0^H f(\xi,\eta) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi \xi}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{m\pi \eta}{H}\right) \,d\eta\,d\xi, \qquad m\geq1. } \]

Here \(\xi\) and \(\eta\) have been used as dummy integration variables to distinguish them from the observation point \((x,y)\).

11. Compact form of the solution

It is convenient to define

\[ \gamma_m= \begin{cases} 1, &m=0,\\ 2, &m\geq1. \end{cases} \]

Then the Fourier coefficients can be written in a single expression:

\[ \boxed{ A_{nm} = \frac{2\gamma_m}{LH} \int_0^L \int_0^H f(\xi,\eta) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi \xi}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{m\pi \eta}{H}\right) \,d\eta\,d\xi. } \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} C(x,y,t) ={}& \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} A_{nm} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \\ &\times \exp \left[ -D\pi^2 \left( \frac{n^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2}{H^2} \right)t \right]. \end{aligned} } \]

This is the required separation-of-variables and Fourier-series solution.

12. Verification of the boundary conditions

Boundary at \(x=0\)

Since

\[ \sin\left(\frac{n\pi(0)}{L}\right)=0, \]

we immediately obtain

\[ C(0,y,t)=0. \]

Boundary at \(x=L\)

Since

\[ \sin\left(\frac{n\pi L}{L}\right) = \sin(n\pi)=0, \]

we obtain

\[ C(L,y,t)=0. \]

Therefore, both Dirichlet boundary conditions are satisfied.

Boundary at \(y=0\)

Differentiate the solution with respect to \(y\):

\[ C_y = -\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} A_{nm} \frac{m\pi}{H} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \sin\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) e^{-\lambda_{nm}t}, \]

where

\[ \lambda_{nm} = D\pi^2 \left( \frac{n^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2}{H^2} \right). \]

At \(y=0\),

\[ \sin\left(\frac{m\pi(0)}{H}\right)=0. \]

Hence

\[ C_y(x,0,t)=0. \]

Similarly, at \(y=H\),

\[ \sin\left(\frac{m\pi H}{H}\right) = \sin(m\pi)=0, \]

and consequently

\[ C_y(x,H,t)=0. \]

Thus all four boundary conditions are satisfied.

13. Physical interpretation

The solution consists of an infinite collection of diffusion modes:

\[ \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right). \]

Each mode decays exponentially according to

\[ \exp \left[ -D\pi^2 \left( \frac{n^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2}{H^2} \right)t \right]. \]

The decay rate is therefore

\[ \boxed{ \lambda_{nm} = D\pi^2 \left( \frac{n^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2}{H^2} \right). } \]

Higher spatial frequencies have larger \(n\) or \(m\), and therefore decay more rapidly.

For example,

\[ \lambda_{11} = D\pi^2 \left( \frac{1}{L^2} + \frac{1}{H^2} \right), \]

while

\[ \lambda_{32} = D\pi^2 \left( \frac{9}{L^2} + \frac{4}{H^2} \right). \]

Hence the \((3,2)\) mode disappears much faster than the \((1,1)\) mode.

The special modes with \(m=0\) decay according to

\[ \exp\left( -\frac{Dn^2\pi^2}{L^2}t \right). \]

Since no concentration can escape through \(y=0\) or \(y=H\), diffusion in the \(y\)-direction does not remove the spatially uniform \(y\) component. Concentration can, however, leave through the absorbing boundaries \(x=0\) and \(x=L\), so every allowed mode eventually decays.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}C(x,y,t)=0. } \]

14. Example with a specific initial concentration

Suppose, for example,

\[ f(x,y)=C_0 \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) \left[ 1+a\cos\left(\frac{2\pi y}{H}\right) \right], \]

where \(C_0\) and \(a\) are constants.

The initial concentration contains only two eigenmodes:

\[ (n,m)=(1,0) \]

and

\[ (n,m)=(1,2). \]

Thus no infinite calculation is necessary.

For the first mode,

\[ A_{10}=C_0. \]

For the second mode,

\[ A_{12}=aC_0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{aligned} C(x,y,t) ={}& C_0 \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) \exp\left( -\frac{D\pi^2}{L^2}t \right) \\ &+ aC_0 \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{2\pi y}{H}\right) \\ &\qquad\times \exp \left[ -D\pi^2 \left( \frac{1}{L^2} + \frac{4}{H^2} \right)t \right]. \end{aligned} \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} C(x,y,t) ={}& C_0 \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) \bigg[ e^{-D\pi^2t/L^2} \\ &\qquad+ a\cos\left(\frac{2\pi y}{H}\right) e^{-D\pi^2(1/L^2+4/H^2)t} \bigg]. \end{aligned} } \]

Since

\[ D\pi^2 \left( \frac{1}{L^2} + \frac{4}{H^2} \right) > \frac{D\pi^2}{L^2}, \]

the \(y\)-dependent part decays more rapidly. At sufficiently large time,

\[ C(x,y,t) \sim C_0 \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) e^{-D\pi^2t/L^2}. \]

Thus the concentration first becomes approximately uniform in the \(y\)-direction and then continues to decay through diffusion toward the absorbing boundaries at \(x=0\) and \(x=L\).

Final result

For the mixed boundary-value problem

\[ C(0,y,t)=C(L,y,t)=0, \]

\[ C_y(x,0,t)=C_y(x,H,t)=0, \]

and

\[ C(x,y,0)=f(x,y), \]

the solution is

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} C(x,y,t) ={}& \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} A_{nm} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{m\pi y}{H}\right) \\ &\times \exp \left[ -D\pi^2 \left( \frac{n^2}{L^2} + \frac{m^2}{H^2} \right)t \right], \end{aligned} } \]

where

\[ \boxed{ A_{n0} = \frac{2}{LH} \int_0^L \int_0^H f(\xi,\eta) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi\xi}{L}\right) \,d\eta\,d\xi } \]

and, for \(m\geq1\),

\[ \boxed{ A_{nm} = \frac{4}{LH} \int_0^L \int_0^H f(\xi,\eta) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi\xi}{L}\right) \cos\left(\frac{m\pi\eta}{H}\right) \,d\eta\,d\xi. } \]

The essential feature of the mixed conditions is therefore that Dirichlet conditions generate sine eigenfunctions, whereas homogeneous Neumann conditions generate cosine eigenfunctions, including the zero mode \(m=0\).

Example - Axisymmetric 2-D diffusion in a cylinder

Level sets of \(C\) diffuse faster along directions of larger eigenvalues. In axisymmetric coordinates \((r,z)\) with \(C=C(r,z,t)\) (no \(\theta\) dependence), \[ \nabla\!\cdot(\mathbf D\nabla C)= \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} \!\left(r\,D_{rr}\frac{\partial C}{\partial r}\right) +\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\left(D_{zz}\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}\right), \] and advection adds \(v_r C_r + v_z C_z\) if \(\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf v=0\).

Consider an axially symmetric problem with \(C=C(r,z,t)\) independent of the azimuthal angle \(\theta\) in cylindrical coordinates \((r,\theta,z)\), over \(0<r<a,\, 0<z<\ell\). The governing equation is \[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = \nabla\!\cdot(D\,\nabla C) \] with \[ \nabla^2 C = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial z^2} \quad (\text{axial symmetry}). \] Specify data such as \(C(r,z,0=f(r,z)\) and boundary conditions \[ C(0,z,t)=f_1(z,t),\quad C(r,0,t)=f_2(r,t),\quad \text{etc.}\]

With constant isotropic diffusivity \(D>0\), the diffusion equation reads \[\begin{equation} \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D\,\nabla^2 C,\quad t>0, \tag{7.8} \end{equation}\] where \[ \nabla^2 C = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial z^2}, \qquad (r,z)\in(0,a)\times(0,\ell). \]

Initial condition and boundary conditions (BCs) must be specified. Below we work out a canonical case (homogeneous Dirichlet at \(r=a\), \(z=0\) and \(z=\ell\)) and list variants.

Assume \[\begin{align} C(a,z,t) &= 0, & 0<z<\ell,\ t>0, \\ C(r,0,t) &= 0, & 0<r<a,\ t>0, \\ C(r,\ell,t) &= 0, & 0<r<a,\ t>0, \\ C(r,z,0) &= f(r,z), & 0<r<a,\ 0<z<\ell. \end{align}\]

We seek a separated solution \(C(r,z,t)=R(r)\,Z(z)\,T(t)\). Substituting into (7.8) and dividing by \(D\) and \(RZT\) yields \[ \frac{1}{D}\frac{T'}{T} = \frac{R''+\tfrac{1}{r}R'}{R} + \frac{Z''}{Z} = -\sigma, \] for some separation constant \(\sigma>0\). Further separating \(r\) and \(z\), \[ Z'' + \mu^2 Z = 0, \qquad R'' + \frac{1}{r}R' + \nu^2 R = 0, \]

with \(\sigma=\mu^2+\nu^2,\) and the time factor \(T\) satisfies \[ T'+D\sigma T=0. \]

Axial eigenproblem (\(z\)-dependence)

Dirichlet at \(z=0,\ell\) gives \[ Z_m(z) = \sin\!\left(\frac{m\pi z}{\ell}\right), \qquad \mu_m=\frac{m\pi}{\ell},\qquad m=1,2,\dots \] with orthogonality \(\displaystyle \int_0^\ell Z_m(z)\,Z_p(z)\,dz = \frac{\ell}{2}\,\delta_{mp}.\)

Radial eigenproblem (\(r\)-dependence)

Regularity at \(r=0\) eliminates \(Y_0\); the bounded solutions are Bessel \(J_0\). The boundary \(R(a)=0\) imposes \[ R_n(r) = J_0\!\left(\frac{\alpha_n r}{a}\right), \qquad \nu_n=\frac{\alpha_n}{a}, \] where \(\alpha_n\) is the \(n\)-th positive zero of \(J_0(\cdot)\): \(J_0(\alpha_n)=0\), \(n=1,2,\dots\) Orthogonality: \[ \int_0^a r\, R_n(r)\,R_p(r)\,dr = \frac{a^2}{2}\,J_1(\alpha_n)^2\,\delta_{np}. \]

Time factor \[ T_n(t) = \exp(-D\sigma_n t),\qquad \sigma_n = \mu_m^2 + \nu_n^2. \]

Expanding the initial data \(f\) in the complete orthogonal set \(\{R_n(r)Z_m(z)\}\) gives

\[\begin{equation} C(r,z,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty} A_{nm}\, J_0\!\left(\frac{\alpha_n r}{a}\right)\, \sin\!\left(\frac{m\pi z}{\ell}\right)\, \exp\!\left(-D\left[\left(\frac{\alpha_n}{a}\right)^2+\left(\frac{m\pi}{\ell}\right)^2\right]t\right). \end{equation}\]

The coefficients \(A_{nm}\) are obtained by orthogonality: \[\begin{equation} A_{nm} = \frac{4}{\ell\,a^2\,J_1(\alpha_n)^2} \int_0^\ell\!\int_0^a f(r,z)\, J_0\!\left(\frac{\alpha_n r}{a}\right)\, \sin\!\left(\frac{m\pi z}{\ell}\right)\, r\,dr\,dz. \end{equation}\]

Example - 2D Filtration Problem

A 2-D filtration problem: a salt solution moves along a duct in the \(x\)-direction, with half-height \(h\) (we model \(0\le y\le h\)), driven by pressure. Water is extracted through semi-permeable membranes at \(y=0\) and \(y=2h\) (symmetry allows reduction to \(0\le y\le h\)). The horizontal velocity profile is approximated as fully developed and linear in \(y\): \[ v_1(y)=u_0\,\frac{y}{h},\quad 0\le y \le h, \qquad v_2(y)=-\,q\quad(\text{constant permeation}). \] The steady convection–diffusion model (neglecting axial diffusion to leading order) becomes \[\begin{equation} u_0\,\frac{y}{h}\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial x} - q\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial y} = D\,\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial y^2}, \qquad x>0,\quad 0<y<h, \tag{7.9} \end{equation}\] subject to \[\begin{align} C(0,y) &= c_0, && 0\le y\le h, \\ -\,D\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial y}(x,0) - q\,C(x,0) &= 0, && x>0 \quad \text{(no salt crosses the membrane)},\\ \frac{\partial C}{\partial y}(x,h)&=0, && x>0 \quad \text{(symmetry at the centerline)}. \end{align}\]

Collecting these gives the half-duct boundary value problem \[ u_0\,\frac{y}{h}\,C_x - q\,C_y = D\,C_{yy}, \] subject to \[ C(0,y)=c_0, \quad D\,C_y(x,0) = -q\,C(x,0), \quad C_y(x,h)=0. \]

Non-dimensionalization

Define the dimensionless variables \[ X=\frac{x}{h},\qquad Y=\frac{y}{h},\qquad Q=\frac{q}{u_0},\quad \alpha=\dfrac{D}{u_0 h} \] and \[ \zeta(X,Y)=\frac{C(x,y)-c_0}{c_0}. \]

Then (7.9) transforms, after division by \(u_0\), to a dimensionless system. Under the assumption that advection in \(x\) dominates advection in \(y\), the simplified leading-order model is \[\begin{equation} \zeta_X \;=\; \alpha\,\zeta_{YY}, \quad X>0,\ 0<Y<1; \tag{7.10} \end{equation}\] subject to

\[ \zeta(0,Y)=0,\quad \zeta_Y(X,0)=-Q\,\zeta(X,0),\quad \zeta_Y(X,1)=0. \]

A General Transformation

Consider the stretching transformations \[\begin{equation} X^*=e^{a}X,\qquad Y^*=e^{b}Y,\qquad \psi=e^{c}\zeta, \tag{7.11} \end{equation}\] and choose \(a,b,c\) so that the PDE and BCs are invariant. One finds \(a=3b\) and \(c=b\), leading to the invariant variable \[\begin{equation} \eta \;=\; \frac{Y}{X^{1/3}}, \end{equation}\] and the similarity form \[\begin{equation} \zeta(X,Y)=X^{1/3}\,f(\eta). \tag{7.12} \end{equation}\] (7.12) into (7.10) gives the ODE \[\begin{equation} \alpha\,f''(\eta)\;-\;\frac{\eta}{3}\,f'(\eta)\;-\;f(\eta)=0, \tag{7.13} \end{equation}\] \[\begin{equation} f(\infty)=0, \qquad f'(0) = -\,\frac{Q}{\alpha}. \tag{7.14} \end{equation}\]

A solution satisfying \(f(\infty)=0\) admits the integral representation \[\begin{equation} f(\eta) = \gamma\,\eta\int_{\eta}^{\infty} \frac{1}{v^2}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha v^{3}}{9}\right)\,dv, \tag{7.15} \end{equation}\] which, after integration by parts, can be written as \[\begin{equation} f(\eta) = -\,\frac{3\gamma}{\alpha}\,\eta\int_{\eta}^{\infty} v\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha v^{3}}{9}\right)\,dv. \tag{7.16} \end{equation}\] Imposing (f’(0)=-Q/) determines (=), where \[\begin{equation} I_0 \;=\; \int_{0}^{\infty} v\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{\alpha v^{3}}{9}\right)\,dv \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) \left(\frac{9}{\alpha}\right)^{2/3}. \tag{7.19} \end{equation}\] Hence \[ \zeta(X,0) \;=\; \frac{3Q}{I_0}\,X^{1/3}, \]

\[ \Rightarrow\qquad \frac{C(x,0)}{c_0} \;=\; 1 + \frac{3Q}{I_0}\,\left(\frac{x}{h}\right)^{1/3}. \]

Numerically, with representative parameters one finds \[ \frac{C(x,0)}{c_0} \approx 1 + 0.009\,x^{1/3} \quad \text{(SI units for } x\text{)}. \]

Example - 2D Unsteady Convected Gaussian plume

Let \(C(x,y,t)\) be the concentration of a passive (conservative) tracer released into an unbounded, quiescent medium with constant, uniform wind \(\mathbf v=(U,0)\) and isotropic molecular (or eddy) diffusivity \(D>0\). The governing PDE and data are \[\begin{align} \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + U\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial x} &= D\,\left(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial y^2}\right), && (x,y)\in\mathbb R^2,\ t>0, \\ C(x,y,0) &= M\,\delta(x-x_0)\,\delta(y-y_0), & \\ \lim_{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\to\infty} &C(x,y,t)= 0, && t>0, \tag{7.8} \end{align}\] where \(M\) is the total mass of the tracer released at \((x_0,y_0)\) at \(t=0\), and \(\delta(\cdot)\) is the Dirac delta function. The far-field condition ensures that the plume vanishes at infinity.

Note. Units: \([C]=\mathrm{mass}/\mathrm{area}\), \([M]=\mathrm{mass}\), \([U]=\mathrm{length}/\mathrm{time}\), \([D]=\mathrm{length}^2/\mathrm{time}\).

Solution by Galilean transformation (co-moving frame)

Introduce the translating coordinate \[ \xi = x - U t,\qquad \eta = y, \] \[ W(\xi,\eta,t) := C(x,y,t)=C(\xi+Ut,\eta,t). \] By the chain rule, \[ \frac{\partial W}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + U\,\frac{\partial C}{\partial x},\qquad \frac{\partial^2 W}{\partial \xi^2}=\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2},\quad \frac{\partial^2 W}{\partial \eta^2}=\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial y^2}. \] Hence, the problembecomes the 2-D heat equation \[ W_t = D\,(W_{\xi\xi}+W_{\eta\eta}),\qquad (\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb R^2,\ t>0, \] with initial condition (from (7.8)\(_2\)) \[ W(\xi,\eta,0)=C(\xi,\eta,0)=M\,\delta(\xi-x_0)\,\delta(\eta-y_0). \] The fundamental (Green’s) solution of the 2-D heat equation is the Gaussian kernel \[ G(\xi,\eta,t)=\frac{1}{4\pi D t}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{\xi^2+\eta^2}{4Dt}\right),\qquad t>0. \] Convolving the initial delta with \(G\) gives

\[\begin{align} W(\xi,\eta,t)&=M\,G(\xi-x_0,\eta-y_0,t)\\ &=\frac{M}{4\pi D t}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\xi-x_0)^2+(\eta-y_0)^2}{4Dt}\right). \end{align}\]

Transforming back to \((x,y)\) (recall \(\xi=x-Ut\)) yields the convected plume, \(t>0\): \[\begin{equation} C(x,y,t) =\frac{M}{4\pi D t}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-x_0-Ut)^2+(y-y_0)^2}{4Dt}\right).\tag{7.9} \end{equation}\]

Verification and properties

  • Mass conservation. Integrating (7.9) over \(\mathbb R^2\), \[\begin{align} &\int_{\mathbb R^2} C(x,y,t)\,dx\,dy \\ &= \frac{M}{4\pi D t}\,\Big( \int_{\mathbb R} e^{-(x-x_0-Ut)^2/(4Dt)}\,dx \Big) \Big( \int_{\mathbb R} e^{-(y-y_0)^2/(4Dt)}\,dy \Big) = M. \end{align}\]

  • Initial condition (distributional limit).

\[ C(\cdot,\cdot,t)\rightharpoonup M\,\delta(x-x_0)\delta(y-y_0)\quad \text{as}\quad t\downarrow 0. \]

  • Mean position and spread. The plume center moves with the wind, \(\langle x\rangle=x_0+Ut\), \(\langle y\rangle=y_0\). The standard deviations grow as \(\sigma_x=\sigma_y=\sqrt{2Dt}\), so the plume spreads isotropically in the crosswind direction while being advected downstream.

Extensions

  • General uniform advection. For \(\mathbf v=(U,V)\), replace \(x_0\) by \(x_0+Ut\) and \(y_0\) by \(y_0+Vt\) in (7.9).

  • Anisotropic diffusion. If the diffusion tensor is a constant SPD matrix \(K\in\mathbb R^{2\times2}\), the kernel becomes \[ C(x,y,t)=\frac{M}{4\pi t\sqrt{\det K}}\, \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{4t}\,(\mathbf r - \mathbf v t)^\top K^{-1}(\mathbf r - \mathbf v t)\Big), \quad \mathbf r=(x-x_0,\ y-y_0)^\top, \] with ellipsoidal contours aligned with the eigenvectors of \(K\).

  • Continuous point source (rate \(Q\)). Superpose impulses: \[ C(x,y,t)=\displaystyle\int_0^t \frac{Q}{4\pi D (t-\tau)} \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{(x-x_0-U(t-\tau))^2+(y-y_0)^2}{4D(t-\tau)}\big)\,d\tau. \] In an unbounded 2-D domain, impulsive point release \(M\) at \((x_0,y_0)\), uniform wind \(\mathbf v=(U,0)\), isotropic \(D\): \[ C(x,y,t) =\frac{M}{4\pi D t}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-x_0-Ut)^2+(y-y_0)^2}{4Dt}\right). \] Contours are circles that translate at speed \(U\) while diffusing.

  • Generalizations to include wind shear, variable diffusivity, and reaction terms can be handled using similar techniques, often leading to more complex Gaussian-like solutions.

  • Extensions to three-dimensional flows can be treated similarly, with the Green’s function becoming a 3-D Gaussian kernel.

  • Coupling with boundary conditions, such as impermeable walls or open boundaries, can be incorporated using image methods or numerical techniques.

Wall Mass Transfer in a Channel

In a channel flow with large \(\mathrm{Pe}\), a thin concentration boundary layer \(\,\delta\ll h\,\) forms. A classical similarity analysis (Lévêque) gives the local wall flux \[ J_w(x)\sim D\,\partial_y c|_{y=0}\propto x^{-1/3} \] and the Sherwood number \[ \mathrm{Sh}(x)=\dfrac{J_w\,h}{D(c_b-c_w)}\propto \mathrm{Re}^{1/3}\mathrm{Sc}^{1/3}(x/h)^{-1/3}, \] linking momentum (\(\mathrm{Re}\)) and mass transfer (\(\mathrm{Sc}=\nu/D\)).

Convection–diffusion problems are governed by a balance between transport by the flow and molecular (or turbulent) smoothing by diffusion. The Péclet number \(\mathrm{Pe}=UL/D\) organizes regimes, analytic approximations, and numerical choices.

Physical setting and assumptions.

Consider steady laminar flow in a straight channel with half-gap \(h\) (wall at \(y=0\), core toward \(y\to\infty\) in a boundary–layer approximation). A dilute, conservative solute has bulk concentration \(c_b\) in the core and an absorbing wall concentration \(c_w\) at \(y=0\) (with \(c_b>c_w\)). Let \(u(y)\) be the streamwise velocity. In the entrance region, the concentration boundary layer thickness \(\delta(x)\ll h\) and the longitudinal diffusion \(\partial^2 c/\partial x^2\) are negligible. Near the wall the velocity is approximately linear: \[ u(y)\;\approx\; S\,y, \qquad S:=\left.\frac{du}{dy}\right|_{y=0} \quad\text{(wall shear rate)}. \] For fully developed plane Poiseuille flow of mean speed \(U\) in a channel of half-gap \(h\), \(S=3U/h\).

Boundary-layer model (Lévêque approximation).

With isotropic molecular diffusivity \(D>0\), the steady advection–diffusion equation becomes \[\begin{equation} S\,y\,\frac{\partial c}{\partial x} \;=\; D\,\frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial y^2}, \qquad x>0,\quad y>0, \tag{7.10} \end{equation}\] with boundary/initial data \[\begin{equation} c(x,0)=c_w,\,\, c(x,y)\to c_b\, (y\to\infty),\,\, c(0,y)=c_b. \tag{7.11} \end{equation}\]

Introduce the dimensionless concentration \(\theta(x,y):=\dfrac{c(x,y)-c_w}{c_b-c_w}\) so that \(\theta(x,0)=0,\ \ \theta(x,\infty)=1,\ \ \theta(0,y)=1.\)

Similarity analysis

Seek a self-similar form \(\theta(x,y)=\Theta(\eta)\) with the similarity variable \[ \eta \;=\; \frac{y}{\delta(x)}, \qquad \delta(x) \;=\; \Big(\frac{3D\,x}{S}\Big)^{1/3}. \] A straightforward calculation gives \[ \theta_x= -\,\Theta'(\eta)\,\eta\,\frac{\delta'(x)}{\delta(x)},\qquad \theta_{yy}=\frac{\Theta''(\eta)}{\delta(x)^2}, \qquad \frac{\delta'(x)}{\delta(x)}=\frac{1}{3x}. \]

Substituting into \(\eqref{7.10}\) yields the ordinary differential equation \[\begin{equation} \Theta''(\eta) + \eta^2\,\Theta'(\eta) \;=\; 0, \quad \Theta(0)=0,\quad \Theta(\infty)=1. \tag{7.12} \end{equation}\]

Let \(\Phi(\eta):=\Theta'(\eta)\). Then \(\Phi'+\eta^2\Phi=0\), so \[ \Phi(\eta)=C\,e^{-\eta^3/3},\qquad \Theta(\eta)=1-C\int_{\eta}^{\infty} e^{-s^3/3}\,ds. \] Imposing \(\Theta(0)=0\) fixes \[ C^{-1}=\int_0^\infty e^{-s^3/3}\,ds = \frac{1}{3}\,\Gamma\!\Big(\frac{1}{3}\Big)\,\Big(\frac{3}{1}\Big)^{1/3} =\frac{\Gamma(1/3)}{3^{2/3}}, \] \[ \Rightarrow\quad C=\frac{3^{2/3}}{\Gamma(1/3)}. \]

Therefore \[ \Theta'(\eta)=\frac{3^{2/3}}{\Gamma(1/3)}\,e^{-\eta^3/3}, \qquad \Theta(0)=0,\ \ \Theta(\infty)=1. \]

Local wall flux, mass-transfer coefficient, Sherwood number
The local diffusive wall flux is \[\begin{aligned} J_w(x)\;=\; -\,D\,\bigg.\frac{\partial c}{\partial y}\bigg|_{y=0} &=\; -\,D\,(c_b-c_w)\,\frac{\Theta'(0)}{\delta(x)}\\ &=\; (c_b-c_w)\,\frac{D\,C}{\delta(x)}, \end{aligned}\]

with \(C=\Theta'(0)=3^{2/3}/\Gamma(1/3)\) and \(\delta(x)=(3Dx/S)^{1/3}\). Hence \[\begin{equation} J_w(x) = \underbrace{\frac{3^{1/3}}{\Gamma(1/3)}}_{\displaystyle \approx\,0.538}\; (c_b-c_w)\;D^{2/3}\,S^{1/3}\,x^{-1/3}. |\tag{7.13} \end{equation}\]

Define the local mass-transfer coefficient \(k_c(x):=J_w(x)/(c_b-c_w)\): \[\begin{equation} k_c(x) = \frac{3^{1/3}}{\Gamma(1/3)}\;D^{2/3}\,S^{1/3}\,x^{-1/3}. \tag{7.14} \end{equation}\]

Two common Sherwood-number normalizations are:

(i) Based on the gap \(h\). \[ \mathrm{Sh}_h(x):=\frac{k_c(x)\,h}{D} = \underbrace{\frac{3^{2/3}}{\Gamma(1/3)}}_{\displaystyle \approx\,0.776}\; \Big(\frac{S\,h^3}{D}\Big)^{1/3}\Big(\frac{h}{x}\Big)^{1/3}. \]

For plane Poiseuille flow with \(S=3U/h\), \[ \mathrm{Sh}_h(x) \simeq 0.776\;\mathrm{Re}^{1/3}\,\mathrm{Sc}^{1/3}\, \Big(\frac{h}{x}\Big)^{1/3}, \quad \mathrm{Re}=\frac{U h}{\nu},\ \ \mathrm{Sc}=\frac{\nu}{D}. \]

(ii) Based on the streamwise distance \(x\). \[ \mathrm{Sh}_x(x):=\frac{k_c(x)\,x}{D} = \frac{3^{1/3}}{\Gamma(1/3)}\;\Big(\frac{S\,x^2}{D}\Big)^{1/3}. \]

Length-averaged coefficient over \([0,L]\)

The average mass-transfer coefficient \(\bar k_c(L)=\dfrac{1}{L}\int_0^L k_c(x)\,dx\) is \[ \bar k_c(L)=\frac{3}{2}\,\frac{3^{1/3}}{\Gamma(1/3)}\;D^{2/3}S^{1/3}L^{-1/3}, \] so the length-averaged Sherwood number based on \(h\) is \[ \overline{\mathrm{Sh}}_h(L) =\frac{\bar k_c(L)\,h}{D} = \underbrace{\frac{3}{2}\,\frac{3^{2/3}}{\Gamma(1/3)}}_{\displaystyle \approx\,1.164}\; \mathrm{Re}^{1/3}\,\mathrm{Sc}^{1/3}\,\Big(\frac{h}{L}\Big)^{1/3}. \]

Validity and remarks
  • Lévêque region: \(\delta(x)\ll h\) so that \(u(y)\approx S y\) and the core concentration remains \(c_b\). This requires \(x\ll x_\ast\), with \[ \delta/h \sim (3Dx/(S h^3))^{1/3}\ll 1 \ \Rightarrow\ x \ll \dfrac{S h^3}{3D} \sim \dfrac{U h^2}{D}. \]
  • High axial Péclet: \(Pe_x=Ux/D\gg 1\) to neglect streamwise diffusion.
  • The numerical coefficients above arise from the exact integral \[ \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-s^3/3}\,ds = \Gamma(1/3)/3^{2/3}. \]
  • For other geometries (e.g. circular tube) or different velocity profiles, only the prefactors change; the \(x^{-1/3}\) and \(Re^{1/3}Sc^{1/3}\) scalings persist.
Concluding Summary

In the Lévêque entrance region of laminar channel flow, the wall flux decays as \(x^{-1/3}\) and the (gap-based) Sherwood number scales as \(\mathrm{Sh}_h(x)\propto \mathrm{Re}^{1/3}\mathrm{Sc}^{1/3}(x/h)^{-1/3}\), with explicit constants given in (7.13)–(7.14).
